Subwords sit between characters and words

UnitVocabularyA new wordLength of a message

A frequent word is one piece. A rare word is several. A word from a language the tokeniser rarely saw is many.

Arabic is rich in forms: one stem takes prefixes and suffixes (the article, prepositions, pronouns), so a word-level vocabulary explodes. Subwords handle this well when the tokeniser was trained on Arabic. The last sentence on the slide is the whole lab.

How a vocabulary is learnt

Byte-pair encoding merges the most frequent pair, again and again

  1. Start from single characters or bytes.
  2. Count every adjacent pair in the training text.
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into one new piece.
  4. Repeat until the vocabulary has the size you asked for.

What the merges remember

The training text. English web text gives English pieces; Arabic text gives Arabic pieces.

WordPiece

The same idea with another score; it marks a continuing piece with ## and has an [UNK] piece.

Byte-pair encoding as used for text comes from Sennrich and colleagues (2016); WordPiece is the variant BERT uses. Two tokenisers in the lab are byte-level BPE, one is WordPiece. Byte-level means the starting alphabet is the 256 bytes, so nothing is ever unknown. The price shows on the next slide.

Sources: Sennrich et al., Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units; Devlin et al., BERT.

Embeddings

An embedding puts a text at a point; close points mean close meanings

  • A token id selects a row of a table: its embedding.
  • A model mixes the rows of a message into one vector for the whole message.
  • Trained so that texts used alike land close together.
  • A multilingual model lands a message and its translation close together.

Close

الماي مقطوع · no water at home · el mai maqtoo3

Far

no water at home · I was charged twice

Keep the geometry simple: a few hundred to a few thousand numbers per text. What the numbers mean individually does not matter; distances do. "Used alike" is the training signal: pairs of texts that belong together (a question and its answer, a sentence and its translation) are pulled together, others pushed apart.

Similarity

With unit vectors, similarity is one dot product

# E: (2000, d), each row of length 1
E = G.embed(messages)
q = G.embed([query])[0]   # (d,)
# cosine similarity, 2000 at once
scores = E @ q
top = np.argsort(-scores)[:10]
  • Cosine similarity compares directions and ignores length: 1 the same, 0 unrelated.
  • Normalise every vector to length 1 once; then cosine is the dot product.
  • One matrix multiplication scores a query against the whole archive.

This is the whole of exact vector search. Everything a vector database adds is about doing this faster on more rows, filtering, and keeping it up to date. Let the room see that the mathematics is four lines.

Attention

Every token asks, every token offers, and each takes a weighted mix

Query

What this token is looking for.

Key

What each token offers to be found by.

Value

What a token hands over when it is chosen.

weights = softmax(Q @ K.T / sqrt(d_k))   # (tokens, tokens): each row sums to 1
output  = weights @ V                     # each token: a weighted average of the values

Say it with the sentence from the lab: in "the meter is stopped although we use water", the word for "stopped" needs to know what is stopped. Its query matches the key of "meter"; it takes most of its mix from that value. Query, key and value are three linear layers applied to the same token vectors. Introduced in "Attention Is All You Need" (2017).

Sources: Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need.

Lab 1.3, step 2

Attention is eight lines, and the lab checks yours against the model's

def attention_weights(q, k, causal=False):
    scores = q @ k.T / math.sqrt(q.shape[-1])
    if causal:      # a decoder may not look ahead
        seen = torch.ones_like(scores).tril().bool()
        scores = scores.masked_fill(~seen, -math.inf)
    return scores.softmax(-1)
  • Dividing by the square root of the head's width keeps the softmax from saturating.
  • The causal mask is the only difference between an encoder's attention and a decoder's.
  • The check: your weights equal the model's own for layer 0, head 0, to five decimals.

Minus infinity before the softmax becomes a weight of exactly zero after it. Point at the one "if": everything else about the two model families follows from that line and from what they were trained to predict.

One mask apart

An encoder reads both ways; a decoder only looks back

Encoder

Every token attends to every token. In the lab's sentence, 36% of the first layer's attention goes to later tokens.

Trained to fill in a hidden token from both sides.

Decoder

A token attends to itself and earlier tokens. Attention to later tokens: exactly 0.

Trained to predict the next token from the left side.

An attention map shows where a head looked. It is not an explanation of the model's decision.

The 36% and the 0 are from the course's run of Lab 1.3, step 3, on the sentence "العداد واقف مع اننا نستخدم الماي". The callout matters: people over-read attention maps. An encoder-decoder model has both stacks; it is used for translation and is not built in this course.

Decision models

Most language work in production is a decision, not a paragraph

Classification

One label for a message: a department, urgent or not, a language variety.

Token classification

One label per word: this is a district, this is an account number.

Ranking

An order over candidates: which earlier messages are most alike.

  • A decision has a right answer, so it can be scored on thousands of examples.
  • It runs on every message, so its latency and cost are multiplied by the volume.

Routing is the decision this course follows through all three approaches. Ask where in the room's own organisations a paragraph-writing model is really doing a classification job at a paragraph's price.
